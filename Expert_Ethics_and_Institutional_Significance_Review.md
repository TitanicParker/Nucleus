# Executive judgment

## Scope and evidential discipline

This review examines a single alleged information-state mechanism: whether an authoritative specialist record could have transformed an unresolved clinical relationship into portable apparent knowledge, transmitted that information state through later care, constrained reconstruction of the founding relationship, changed the later meaning of the patient's own testimony, and generated documentary descendants later capable of appearing to validate the original construction. The mechanism is analysed at full ethical strength, but it is not treated as factually established. No underlying clinical file, audit trail, witness evidence, or independent expert report was supplied for this review; the case-specific propositions therefore remain allegations or analytical premises unless and until independently evidenced.

The analysis uses a strict evidence-status vocabulary throughout:

| Status | Meaning in this review |
|---|---|
| Documentary fact | A proposition proved directly by an authenticated contemporaneous source. |
| Patient evidence | An account or recollection attributable to the patient, requiring assessment rather than automatic acceptance or rejection. |
| Assumed premise | A proposition temporarily treated as true so its ethical implications can be analysed. |
| Reasonable inference | A conclusion supported by established facts but not directly recorded. |
| Clinical-expert question | A proposition requiring appropriate specialist expertise and contemporaneous-standard analysis. |
| Provenance question | A question about authorship, timing, source, copying, version history, or transformation of information. |
| Ethical conclusion | A normative judgment conditional on the evidential foundation stated. |
| Regulatory question | A matter whose legal/regulatory relevance depends on a body's statutory remit and evidential threshold. |
| Legal question | A question requiring applicable law, limitation rules, jurisdiction, procedure, and legal advice. |
| Subjective intent | A proposition about motive or consciousness; never inferred merely from documentary effect. |

The central conclusion is conditional but strong: **if the mechanism were substantially established, the ethical wrong would not be exhausted by diagnostic error. It would concern the integrity of an information-producing act performed under specialist authority.** The alleged defect would be a mismatch between the actual epistemic state and the epistemic status communicated to future readers. That mismatch could exist even where individual ingredients were true, a neuropathy differential was medically possible, structural foot pathology existed, Parkinsonism was correctly recognised, and later clinicians acted conscientiously.

A useful distinction is therefore:

> **Clinical uncertainty**: uncertainty that remains after proportionate specialist investigation and is represented as such.
>
> **Unearned documentary uncertainty**: uncertainty that remains because a material relationship was not adequately worked through, but is transmitted in a form that reasonably invites later readers to believe the question received sufficient specialist consideration.

The second is ethically more serious because a specialist discharge or clinic synthesis is not only retrospective description. It is a prospective instrument of care. Medical-record scholarship has long treated records as constitutive components of clinical work rather than neutral mirrors of it; records coordinate later action, categories and interpretations.[REF-001](#ref-001) Empirical work also shows that wording in notes can influence subsequent clinicians' attitudes and decisions even where medically relevant facts are held constant.[REF-002](#ref-002) This supports the proposition that documentary architecture can itself be a patient-safety variable.

## Bottom-line institutional ranking

**Highest priority, once a minimum evidential package exists:** the relevant hospital/service clinical governance and Quality & Patient Safety (QPS) structures; the relevant HSE Health Region; and, if the evidence supports serious doctor-specific professional-conduct or performance concerns, the Medical Council. HSE's current national QPS functions expressly include incident management, open disclosure, NIMS, clinical audit, safety intelligence and learning.[REF-003](#ref-003) The Medical Council can investigate serious complaints about individual registered doctors and impose registration consequences, but it is not a mechanism for obtaining compensation, compelling an apology, or ordering medical-record changes.[REF-004](#ref-004)

**Narrow but potentially important:** the Data Protection Commission (DPC), where the issue is a demonstrable factual inaccuracy, misattribution, or incompleteness of personal data rather than a request that the DPC decide whether a diagnosis was clinically correct. The DPC expressly states that it will not determine the correctness of a medical diagnosis, while Article 16 may permit completion of incomplete data by a supplementary statement.[REF-005](#ref-005)

**System-intelligence or administrative routes, not substitutes for clinical adjudication:** HIQA can receive and assess information about services against regulations and standards, but states that it has no legal power to investigate individual complaints; the Ombudsman can examine maladministration, wrong information and complaint handling, but cannot examine clinical judgments such as diagnosis or treatment.[REF-006](#ref-006)[REF-007](#ref-007)

**Support rather than adjudication:** the Patient Advocacy Service is independent, free and confidential and can support complaints concerning public acute hospitals and the aftermath of patient-safety incidents.[REF-008](#ref-008)

**Policy and scholarly audiences:** the Department of Health's National Patient Safety Office (NPSO), Council of Europe CDBIO, WHO, WMA, UNESCO bioethics structures, ISQua, diagnostic-safety organisations, health-informatics communities, and medical-ethics/patient-safety researchers are relevant to a mature, de-identified, generalisable mechanism. They are not forums for deciding the individual historical complaint.

## The most important safeguard against hindsight

Current standards can demonstrate the present importance of accurate, relevant, timely, well-governed health information, but they cannot simply be projected backward as the binding standard in 2017. HIQA's National Standards for Information Management in Health and Social Care were approved in 2024, for example.[REF-009](#ref-009) The present HSE Open Disclosure Policy dates from 2025 and implements the contemporary statutory framework.[REF-010](#ref-010) A disciplinary assessment of a 2017 event must reconstruct the rules, knowledge and reasonable specialist practice at that time.

That contemporaneous baseline nevertheless included an explicit Irish Medical Council obligation to keep accurate and up-to-date patient records: the 2016 eighth edition of the professional guide stated that records must be accurate, current, legible and clear, with author/date and appropriate time.[REF-011](#ref-011) The later PRKN result is likewise not a licence for hindsight. Its proper ethical function is narrower: it increases the retrospective biological salience of the lower-limb dystonia relationship. Importantly, lower-limb/foot dystonia in parkin disease was not a concept invented after 2017; pre-2017 literature described early lower-limb dystonia as characteristic and a 2016 study expressly examined the role of dystonia in gait abnormalities in parkin disease.[REF-012](#ref-012)[REF-013](#ref-013) The clinical question remains what a competent neurologist reasonably should have investigated and documented in the actual 2017 context.

# Part I — The ethical wrong alleged

## 1. Epistemic integrity

The primary alleged wrong is **misrepresentation of epistemic status**. Clinical documentation ethically communicates more than conclusions. It communicates what was observed, what was reported, what was inferred, what was excluded, what remains open, and—implicitly—how much epistemic work lies behind a statement such as “not clear.” If the unresolved feet–neurological-sign relationship had not been adequately examined, but the final specialist synthesis made it appear that the relationship had been considered to a degree sufficient to support closure, the defect would concern truthful representation of the state of knowledge.

This matters even if no isolated sentence were demonstrably false. Information can mislead through selection, recoding, juxtaposition, causal weighting, omitted context, and asymmetric preservation of hypotheses. Outside medicine, behavioural research uses the term “paltering” for the strategic use of truthful statements to create a misleading impression; that literature is useful only as an analogy because paltering entails intention, which this review does not assume. The ethically safer clinical formulation is **truthful ingredients, misleading architecture**.

## 2. Professional honesty and candour

Professional honesty includes not merely refraining from fabrication but avoiding documentation that overstates what is known or understates what remains unresolved. A specialist can be legitimately uncertain. The ethical concern arises if authoritative wording converts *insufficiently worked-out uncertainty* into *apparently earned specialist uncertainty*. The distinction is important because later clinicians cannot directly observe the original cognitive work; they infer it from the record.

The WMA International Code of Medical Ethics provides a useful global comparator: it states that physicians must ensure accurate and timely medical documentation.[REF-015](#ref-015) In Ireland, the contemporaneous professional guide similarly required accurate and up-to-date records.[REF-011](#ref-011) These duties do not themselves decide the disputed facts, but they establish that documentary integrity is a core professional obligation rather than an administrative afterthought.

## 3. Autonomy, participation and testimonial justice

If a patient expressly challenged the unresolved relationship before discharge, that challenge was itself clinically relevant information. Preserving it would not require accepting the patient's causal theory. It would preserve the fact that the patient did not understand the central presenting problem to have been resolved and that the adequacy of the inquiry was contemporaneously contested.

Carel and Kidd's work on epistemic injustice in healthcare identifies two relevant vulnerabilities: testimonial injustice, where a patient's credibility is unjustly downgraded, and hermeneutical injustice, where a patient's experience is difficult to render intelligible within dominant clinical categories. They also emphasise the epistemic privilege of healthcare practitioners and institutions.[REF-014](#ref-014) The present mechanism is potentially more structurally specific: the record can alter not only whether a patient's testimony is believed, but what later testimony *appears historically to be*. If the earlier protest disappears, a later repetition may look like new resistance rather than longstanding unresolved concern.

## 4. Fiduciary and relational responsibility

Specialist care creates a relationship of dependence on expertise. A patient cannot independently audit the diagnostic search space or the meanings later clinicians will attach to a specialist summary. That asymmetry supports a duty of **relational stewardship**: where two clinically important phenomena may be causally linked, ownership of the *relationship* should not disappear merely because each phenomenon can be assigned a separate local pathway.

Relational stewardship is not a demand for diagnostic omniscience. It is a duty to preserve ownership of an unresolved clinically important relationship until it has been proportionately assessed, explicitly transferred, or honestly left open.

## 5. Patient safety and continuity

Patient safety literature increasingly treats information quality as a safety determinant. WHO's Global Patient Safety Action Plan 2021–2030 is explicitly directed to reducing avoidable harm and strengthening learning across health systems.[REF-016](#ref-016) HSE's current QPS architecture similarly includes incident management, safety intelligence, clinical audit and system learning.[REF-003](#ref-003)

The alleged mechanism is therefore safety-relevant even without a single dramatic adverse event. It can influence what questions later clinicians regard as already answered, what referrals seem indicated, what histories are repeated, and what evidence is considered surprising. This is a **continuity-of-reasoning** problem, not just a continuity-of-data problem.

## 6. Specialist authority as information-producing power

A specialist summary carries authority because downstream clinicians reasonably assume that specialty-relevant possibilities have been considered to a specialty-appropriate depth. The ethical burden on the author therefore rises with the document's expected portability and authority. A discharge synthesis can redistribute future attention and the burden of proof: a hypothesis encoded as a plausible specialist category may require little effort to preserve, while an omitted or underdeveloped alternative may require substantial new evidence to reopen.

This yields a central proposition:

> **A medical record does not merely preserve knowledge. It governs the future distribution of doubt.**

Berg and Bowker's sociology of the medical record provides a close theoretical analogue: the record does not simply represent a patient's history; it participates in materially rewriting and coordinating clinical realities.[REF-001](#ref-001) The proposed mechanism adds an explicitly normative dimension: because documentation coordinates future doubt, the author has prospective obligations to represent the epistemic state faithfully.

# Part II — Omission of the Protest

For this analytical branch, assume the Protest occurred substantially as described. Its absence from the surviving record must not be used as circular proof that it did not occur; that would simply reproduce the informational effect under examination.

## The Protest as an epistemic warning label

An explicit pre-discharge challenge that “the feet remain unresolved” would perform at least six functions in a record:

1. it would preserve the patient's contemporaneous non-agreement with the apparent resolution;
2. it would warn later clinicians that the adequacy of the relational assessment had been disputed before the record was finalised;
3. it would distinguish *uncertainty after adequate investigation* from *uncertainty still contested as inadequately investigated*;
4. it would preserve an alternative historical interpretation of later dissent;
5. it would give complaint and governance reviewers a contemporaneous reason to inspect the relational reasoning rather than assume closure; and
6. it would preserve the health system's memory of its own uncertainty.

If omitted, the ethical effect can be described as **epistemic warning-label removal**. This is not automatically misconduct: omissions can be inadvertent, judgments about relevance can be reasonable, and memory can be contested. But if the omitted protest was material to the central unresolved problem and the authoritative record was still being completed, its omission would be ethically significant because it changes the information state inherited by people who were not present.

## Patient agreement and later credibility

A record that contains clinician uncertainty but not the patient's contemporaneous challenge can create an appearance of greater shared understanding than actually existed. Years later, the patient's effort to reassert the original relationship can then be reframed—without deliberate bad faith by later clinicians—as resistance to an established diagnosis. The ethical harm is thus partly temporal: deletion of an earlier dissent changes the apparent age and meaning of later dissent.

This connects directly with testimonial injustice. However, the distinctive claim is not simply that the patient was disbelieved. It is that documentary omission may alter the *historical credentials* of the patient's testimony.

# Part III — The microconstruction

The phrase cluster should be analysed cumulatively, not atomistically. The alleged sequence is: structural foot pathology / pain; “burning”; “leg pain”; “markedly divorced”; genuine alcohol exposure; possible secondary peripheral neuropathy; “Not clear as this has improved”; and PARK9 framed as “the neuropathy question.”

## What the architecture could accomplish

If the provenance and contextual meaning of those phrases were established as alleged, the cluster could perform six linked functions:

- **Topological recoding:** “leg pain” may move the historical object away from specifically painful feet and their structural/postural relationship.
- **Sensory recoding:** “burning” supplies a quality compatible with neuropathic reasoning.
- **Causal separation:** “markedly divorced” may convert a patient's phenomenological distinction between tension and pain into a stronger causal separation than the patient intended.
- **Etiological support:** genuine alcohol exposure can supply a plausible acquired neuropathy cause.
- **Category formation:** “possible secondary peripheral neuropathy” converts the object into an authorised specialist differential.
- **Future preservation:** “not clear as this has improved” and a named “neuropathy question” permit the hypothesis to survive uncertainty and acquire a future position in specialist reasoning.

No single move must be false for the collective architecture to matter. The ethical question is whether the final synthesis fairly represented the evidential weight, provenance and unresolved alternatives.

## Asymmetric explanatory futures

The most important comparative test is not “was neuropathy possible?” It is whether competing explanations received proportionate **explanatory infrastructure**. If an unproved peripheral hypothesis received vocabulary, causes, categories and future diagnostic positions, while an observed dystonic-looking lower-limb phenomenon received no equivalent relational pathway connecting posture, gait, loading and painful feet, the architecture may have been asymmetric.

This does not require equal word counts. It requires proportional epistemic treatment. An observed sign and a speculative hypothesis can properly receive different attention; the question is whether the asymmetry reflected evidence or whether the documentation itself created a durable imbalance in what later readers would regard as open.

## Close analogues in literature

Three literatures are especially relevant.

First, research on record language demonstrates that medically equivalent facts can have different downstream effects depending on wording. In a randomized vignette study, trainees who read a stigmatizing note developed more negative attitudes and selected less aggressive pain treatment than those reading a neutral note.[REF-002](#ref-002)

Second, EHR copy-forward research shows that provenance and repeated text matter for patient safety. A systematic review recommended making copied material identifiable and preserving its provenance because reused information can propagate outdated or inaccurate content.[REF-017](#ref-017)

Third, sociology of medical records treats documentation as active infrastructure in clinical work.[REF-001](#ref-001)

Together, these sources support the possibility of documentary causation. They do **not** prove that the disputed phrases in this case had the alleged meaning or were intentionally constructed. That requires source-level provenance, context and expert assessment.

# Part IV — Specialist documentary authority

## Prospective obligations of an authoritative synthesis

A specialist discharge/clinic synthesis can reasonably be expected to:

- identify the principal problems addressed;
- distinguish observation from inference;
- preserve clinically material unresolved questions;
- accurately attribute patient statements and clinician interpretations;
- avoid suggesting that a relationship was tested when it was not;
- communicate what follow-up is required and by whom; and
- enable future clinicians to understand the degree of confidence attached to key propositions.

Because later readers rely on this document without observing the original encounter, the summary's author has a prospective duty of **epistemic fidelity**. This duty is stronger where the document is likely to become the authoritative entry point to years of subsequent care.

## Presumptive closure

Specialist records can create rational presumptions. A GP or later clinician ordinarily cannot reopen every possibility from first principles. They rely on the division of professional labour. This reliance is not itself bias; it is how complex health systems function. The ethical risk appears when the documentary signal of “already considered” is stronger than the underlying work justifies.

A record can therefore transfer not only information but **burdens of reopening**. The more authoritative the document, the more evidence a later actor may feel is required to revisit its architecture.

# Part V — Constrained corrigibility

The proposed distinction—**high local degrees of freedom, low global degrees of freedom**—is coherent and useful.

A system can be visibly responsive while leaving the founding causal map intact. Symptoms can be relabelled, medications adjusted, new manifestations acknowledged, referrals changed, and even contradictions documented. Yet each adaptation can be made *inside* the inherited architecture. The system becomes corrigible at the level of local facts while remaining resistant to reconstruction at the level of causal organisation.

## Ordinary analogues and the conceptual extension

**Diagnostic momentum** describes the increasing acceptance of a diagnostic label as it passes from clinician to clinician. **Anchoring** describes inadequate adjustment from an initial hypothesis. **Belief perseverance** and **path dependence** describe persistence of earlier commitments. **Copy-forward** and **problem-list inertia** describe documentary persistence. **Epistemic injustice** describes credibility and interpretive disadvantage.

Constrained corrigibility differs by focusing on the *distribution of permissible change*: the same system may have many ways to update local content while having few pathways to question the founding architecture. The closest organisational-learning analogue is the distinction between single-loop and double-loop learning. A systematic review of incident-reporting systems found some evidence of single-loop changes to processes but little evidence of double-loop changes to underlying mindsets or latent managerial conditions.[REF-018](#ref-018)

That makes “constrained corrigibility” a useful extension rather than a wholly unprecedented phenomenon. It imports a known learning distinction into longitudinal clinical information states and makes the unit of analysis the causal map itself.

## Practical test for constrained corrigibility

A retrospective review should ask:

1. When later evidence crossed the original partition, was the *partition* explicitly reconsidered?
2. Were raw earlier observations re-read independently, or merely reinterpreted within the inherited labels?
3. Did any later clinician formulate a competing integrated model and compare it with the founding model?
4. Were copied or derivative statements distinguished from independently generated evidence?
5. Did new treatment response trigger causal-map review, or only symptom-level adaptation?
6. Were the patient's earlier concerns restored to the timeline once later evidence made them newly salient?

If the answer is repeatedly “local adjustment without map reconstruction,” constrained corrigibility becomes empirically testable rather than rhetorical.

# Part VI — Recursive institutional validation

The proposed recursive sequence is:

> **founding record creates categories → later care occurs within those categories → later documentation records that care → accumulated documentation is later invoked as evidence that the founding categories were sound.**

This can be legitimate longitudinal confirmation when downstream evidence is sufficiently independent and discriminating. It becomes **endogenous validation** where the founding model materially shaped the observations, terminology, referral pathways, investigations or treatment choices that later appear to corroborate it.

## Independence is a property, not a date

Evidence does not become independent merely because it was recorded years later. A later note that copies, assumes or operationalises an earlier label may be derivative. Conversely, a later examination can be genuinely independent if the clinician re-evaluates primary phenomena without relying on the challenged classification.

The repository should therefore classify each item as:

- **independent** — generated without material dependence on the challenged founding proposition;
- **partially inherited** — contains new observation but interpretation is shaped by inherited categories;
- **derivative** — mainly restates or operationalises earlier documentation;
- **unknown** — provenance or cognitive dependence cannot yet be determined.

## A competent retrospective review protocol

To avoid validating an information architecture with its own descendants, a review should:

1. preserve and authenticate the original source corpus;
2. reconstruct authorship, timestamps, drafts, amendments, audit logs and the first appearance of key phrases;
3. separate raw observations from interpretive labels;
4. identify all downstream copying, paraphrase and unexamined inheritance;
5. obtain an independent movement-disorder expert view of what was clinically reasonable in 2017, using contemporaneous knowledge and without assuming the 2026 genotype was knowable;
6. ask a counterfactual question: **what evidence for the founding partition remains if the founding summary and its documentary descendants are withheld?**;
7. examine whether later clinicians ever globally reconstructed the feet–dystonia relationship rather than locally modifying symptoms;
8. test alternative innocent explanations for every disputed semantic move; and
9. record evidence that weakens the mechanism with the same discipline as evidence that strengthens it.

This is the practical difference between a review that is robust under independent testing and one that merely finds the inherited record internally coherent.

# Part VII — Irish institutional map

## Routing principle

Ireland does not have one body with jurisdiction over the entire alleged mechanism. The mechanism must be decomposed by **mandate**, while remaining conceptually one allegation. The same evidence can legitimately be presented differently to a provider, regulator, ombudsman or data-protection authority because each is being asked a different question.

The HSE now operates through six Health Regions, each responsible for hospital and community care in its area, with its own leadership and local decision-making; the HSE remains a single organisation and the HSE Centre retains national standard-setting responsibility.[REF-019](#ref-019) The relevant route is therefore the actual hospital/service and its present Health Region, not an assumed legacy Hospital Group.

## A. Relevant hospital/service and HSE Health Region — **High priority**

**Mandate.** The treating provider has the most direct capacity to reconstruct its own record, interview staff where feasible, access local systems, audit trails and policies, examine complaint handling, and implement corrective clinical or governance action. Regional structures have responsibility for delivery and accountability across hospital and community services.[REF-019](#ref-019)

**Individual case?** Yes, through the provider's complaints, clinical-governance, records, risk and incident processes.

**System investigation?** Yes, within the service/region, particularly through QPS, clinical audit and incident-management structures.

**Consequences.** Provider-level actions can include explanation, record supplementation/correction where appropriate, renewed clinical review, governance findings, learning actions, policy changes and staff performance processes. The exact powers depend on the employing/service structure and applicable procedure.

**What falls within remit.** The chronology of the admission; authorship and completion of the discharge synthesis; whether the patient's pre-discharge challenge was received and handled; record provenance; whether the relational question was investigated; whether subsequent care inherited the formulation; whether a contemporary independent review is required; and whether an incident/learning process is indicated.

**What does not automatically follow.** A provider review should not presume deliberate manipulation, professional misconduct, negligence or legal liability merely from the proposed architecture.

**Engagement threshold.** Obtain the core source corpus first: referral, admission notes, ward notes, specialist examinations, podiatry/orthopaedic material, discharge drafts/final letter, correspondence, subsequent records that bear on the disputed relationship, and any available metadata/audit trail. The alleged Protest should be supported by the best available contemporaneous or near-contemporaneous evidence.

**Best form.** A structured **request for independent clinical-governance and information-provenance review**, with a proposition ledger and an explicit request that reviewers classify downstream evidence by independence rather than relying on chart volume.

## B. HSE National Quality and Patient Safety / QPS Incident Management — **High for systemic learning once evidenced**

HSE National QPS states that its functions include clinical audit, improvement and education; incident management, open disclosure and NIMS; QPS intelligence; and patient-safety learning.[REF-003](#ref-003) QPS Incident Management develops frameworks and processes to identify, report, manage and learn from safety incidents and to share learning across the system.[REF-020](#ref-020)

The current Incident Management Framework is a natural methodological home for questions about independent healthcare-record review, system analysis, incident learning and the difference between local correction and system learning. It is not an alternative fitness-to-practise tribunal.

**Best ask.** Not “decide that the 2017 clinician was unethical.” Instead: **test whether this is a patient-safety mechanism in which an inherited information state constrained later recognition and whether present incident-review methods adequately detect documentary descendants and non-independent longitudinal evidence.**

## C. HSE Open Disclosure structures — **Medium–High if the matter is accepted as a patient-safety incident or review finding**

The HSE's 2025 Open Disclosure Policy is designed around open, honest, timely and transparent communication following patient-safety or notifiable incidents, with organisational responsibility for governance and assurance.[REF-010](#ref-010) The Patient Safety (Notifiable Incidents and Open Disclosure) Act 2023 commenced on 26 September 2024 and creates mandatory disclosure duties for specified notifiable incidents; broader open disclosure also operates outside the statutory notifiable list.[REF-021](#ref-021)

This current framework should be used carefully. It may govern how a present-day review communicates newly identified patient-safety findings; it is not evidence that a 2017 event was a statutory notifiable incident under a law that did not yet operate.

## D. Medical Council of Ireland — **High if serious doctor-specific evidence survives the historic-case threshold**

The Medical Council's role is to safeguard the public by maintaining standards of professional conduct, performance and skills and by considering complaints about individual registered doctors.[REF-004](#ref-004) It can investigate serious concerns and, where statutory grounds are made out, impose or facilitate registration consequences. Its published examples include serious failure to examine a patient properly, misdiagnosis, fraud/dishonesty and clinical incompetence. It also expressly notes that historic incidents may be closed where investigation is no longer practicable because of the passage of time.[REF-004](#ref-004)

For complaints made on or after 6 May 2025, the amended process begins with a complaint to the CEO; in most cases an Authorised Officer investigates and may request records, interview people and consult experts before Preliminary Proceedings Committee review.[REF-022](#ref-022)

**What the Council can examine here.** Doctor-specific professional conduct/performance: record accuracy, attribution, seriousness of any failure to examine or preserve a material unresolved issue, and—only if evidence supports it—dishonesty or other misconduct.

**What it cannot do.** The Council states that it cannot award compensation, require an apology, provide treatment, order access to records, or order a doctor to change medical records.[REF-004](#ref-004)

**Threshold.** A regulator-facing submission should be much narrower than the ethics dossier. It should contain authenticated primary records, a precise chronology, clearly separated allegations, evidence of authorship/responsibility, and an independent expert opinion on the 2017 clinical standard where clinical adequacy is central. Because the incident is historic, the submission should explain why investigation remains practicable and what evidence is still available.

## E. HIQA — **Medium for service-level safety intelligence; low for individual adjudication**

HIQA invites concerns about health and social care services but expressly states that it does not have legal power to investigate individual complaints. It reviews information and concerns against regulations and standards; serious risk can prompt action, and HIQA can undertake or be directed by the Minister to undertake healthcare safety/quality investigations.[REF-006](#ref-006)

HIQA's 2024 National Standards for Information Management in Health and Social Care are highly relevant prospectively: HIQA describes high-quality information as foundational to safe, high-quality care and emphasises information governance and accurate, relevant, timely information.[REF-009](#ref-009) A 2025 consultation draft for a National Standard for Hospital Discharge Information similarly treated discharge data as essential to continuity of care.[REF-023](#ref-023) Unless superseded by a final standard, that 2025 document should be described as a draft, not binding current law.

**Best ask.** A de-identified or carefully scoped system-safety communication explaining a potential information-management failure mode and why existing standards might need implementation guidance, assurance methods or thematic attention. Do not ask HIQA to decide whether one neurologist's diagnosis was negligent.

## F. Office of the Ombudsman — **Medium, confined to administration and complaint handling**

The Ombudsman can examine unfair decisions, failure to give reasons, delay, wrong information and deficient complaint handling, but cannot examine clinical judgments such as diagnosis or treatment by or on behalf of the HSE.[REF-007](#ref-007) The Office has nevertheless distinguished clinical judgment from administrative actions by healthcare staff that can be examined where they do not themselves involve clinical judgment.[REF-024](#ref-024)

**Potentially within remit.** How a public body handled the complaint; whether it relied on wrong or incomplete administrative information; whether it failed to address a distinct records/process issue; whether reasons were adequate; and whether its review process was procedurally fair.

**Outside remit.** Asking the Ombudsman to decide that neuropathy was clinically implausible, dystonia caused the pain, or the 2017 diagnosis was wrong.

## G. Data Protection Commission — **Medium–High for a precise record-completeness or factual-accuracy issue; otherwise low**

The DPC's medical-record guidance is unusually important here because it draws the exact jurisdictional line. A later change in diagnosis does not automatically make the earlier clinical opinion “inaccurate”; the DPC states that it will not decide whether a diagnosis was correct. But Article 16 can support completion of incomplete data, including by a supplementary statement, depending on the processing context.[REF-005](#ref-005)

A viable data-protection issue would therefore need to identify the **data proposition**: for example, a demonstrably inaccurate attribution to the patient; a factual statement that the patient said X when reliable evidence shows Y; an omitted material patient statement that renders the relevant personal data incomplete for its processing purpose; or refusal to append an appropriate supplementary statement. It should not be framed as a back-door appeal on diagnostic merits.

The first step is ordinarily a documented request to the controller, followed by a DPC complaint if necessary, with evidence of the request and response.[REF-005](#ref-005)

## H. Patient Advocacy Service — **High practical value; no adjudicative power**

The Patient Advocacy Service is independent, free and confidential, and supports people making formal complaints about public acute hospital care and people in the aftermath of patient-safety incidents.[REF-008](#ref-008) It can help structure correspondence and navigate HSE complaint stages, but it does not make regulatory findings.

This is a strong practical route for ensuring the allegation is expressed proportionately and that the patient's voice is not lost in a highly technical dossier.

## I. Department of Health / National Patient Safety Office — **Medium after system significance is evidenced**

The NPSO provides national patient-safety policy and leadership, works on legislation, surveillance, clinical effectiveness and policy/governance, and liaises with the HSE on patient-safety incidents as required.[REF-025](#ref-025) It is not the first-line investigator of an individual historical clinical dispute.

A mature submission should therefore present **a validated failure mode**, not a grievance: how specialist records can create self-reinforcing information states, what current assurance methods may miss, and what policy or national learning response is proposed.

## J. RCPI and postgraduate training bodies — **Medium for professional learning; low for case adjudication**

RCPI operates professional competence schemes and educational infrastructure; the current Maintenance of Professional Competence framework emphasises ongoing competence, reflection and quality improvement.[REF-026](#ref-026) That creates a legitimate educational audience for questions about documentary epistemic integrity, diagnostic stewardship and double-loop learning.

RCPI should not be asked to adjudicate an individual complaint merely because a doctor is a Fellow/member or trained in its system. Where HSE incident review requires specialty expertise, the HSE notes that external specialist support can be sourced through the Forum of Irish Postgraduate Medical Training Bodies.[REF-020](#ref-020)

## K. University / teaching governance, including University of Galway — **Low unless a real institutional nexus is proved**

University governance becomes relevant only if there is evidence that the clinical episode, records or subsequent case material were used in teaching, research, a university-controlled service, supervision, assessment or another activity within university responsibility. A clinician's academic affiliation alone is insufficient.

Research-ethics and research-integrity mechanisms should be engaged only if research, educational reuse or research misconduct is actually in the facts. Ordinary clinical documentation disputes should not be relabelled as research-integrity cases.

## L. Professional indemnity / medico-legal ethics organisations — **Low–Medium advisory relevance**

Medical defence and indemnity organisations can advise their members on record keeping, disclosure, complaints and professional obligations. They are not public adjudicators of a patient's allegations. Their published guidance can, however, help establish the profession's understanding of contemporaneous record obligations; for example, Medical Protection quotes the 2016 Medical Council requirement that patient records be accurate and up to date.[REF-011](#ref-011)

# Part VIII — European institutional map

## European Data Protection Board — **Policy/guidance relevance, no individual complaint jurisdiction**

The EDPB is not a supervisory authority and has no investigative, corrective or sanctioning powers. It explicitly says that it does not handle complaints or investigations; individuals should approach the relevant national data-protection authority or courts.[REF-027](#ref-027)

The mechanism could matter to the EDPB only at a general guidance level—for example, how Article 16 completion/supplementary statements should operate for contested clinical narratives in interoperable EHRs. An individual Irish case belongs first with the controller and the Irish DPC.

## European Commission health functions — **Policy relevance, not general clinical-complaint jurisdiction**

EU health policy can support cooperation, safety, digital-health rules and cross-border initiatives, but Article 168(7) TFEU preserves Member State responsibility for defining health policy and organising/delivering health services and medical care.[REF-028](#ref-028) The Commission should therefore not be portrayed as a general appellate body for Irish clinical care.

The European Health Data Space Regulation is relevant to the future standards watch because it strengthens EU infrastructure for access to and use of electronic health data and contemplates rectification under data-protection law; its implementation is staged rather than an immediate substitute for existing national/GDPR mechanisms.[REF-029](#ref-029)

## European Union Agency for Fundamental Rights — **Research/policy relevance; no complaints**

FRA provides evidence and advice on fundamental rights within EU competence. Its founding framework deliberately excludes dealing with individual complaints.[REF-030](#ref-030)

A de-identified mechanism might interest FRA where it intersects with data protection, access to justice, dignity or rights in digital health, but engagement would be policy/research communication rather than an application for adjudication.

## Council of Europe CDBIO and the Oviedo framework — **High normative relevance, no ordinary individual complaint route**

The Council of Europe's Steering Committee for Human Rights in the fields of Biomedicine and Health (CDBIO) develops normative and policy work at the intersection of biomedicine, health and human rights.[REF-031](#ref-031) This makes it a strong conceptual audience for questions about patient participation, health information, professional authority and digital/AI-era biomedical governance.

The Convention on Human Rights and Biomedicine (Oviedo Convention) is an important bioethical comparator, but jurisdiction must be stated precisely. Ireland is not listed as a signatory or ratifying state on the Council of Europe's treaty status page for Convention No. 164.[REF-032](#ref-032) It should not be cited as binding Irish law on that basis.

## European Court of Human Rights — **Low and highly conditional**

The ECtHR is a judicial mechanism for alleged violations of Convention rights by states, not a medical-ethics tribunal or factual appeal from clinical reviews. Applications face strict admissibility rules, including exhaustion of domestic remedies and a four-month time limit from the final domestic decision, subject to the detailed case law.[REF-033](#ref-033)

Only a lawyer assessing a concrete Convention right, state responsibility and procedural history should evaluate this route. The ethical mechanism's seriousness does not itself create ECtHR jurisdiction.

## European professional and academic networks — **Medium–High scholarly relevance**

The European Society for Philosophy of Medicine and Health Care (ESPMH) exists to develop philosophy of medicine and healthcare and connect scholars across Europe.[REF-034](#ref-034) The European Association of Centres of Medical Ethics (EACME) supports teaching, research, communication and debate on ethical issues in healthcare practice, policy and biomedical science.[REF-035](#ref-035) The European Federation for Medical Informatics (EFMI) promotes high standards, research and education in medical informatics.[REF-036](#ref-036)

These are unusually good audiences for the *general mechanism* because it sits precisely across philosophy of medicine, clinical ethics, health-information governance and longitudinal epistemology. None should be approached as if it could decide the individual complaint.

# Part IX — Global institutional and ethical map

## World Health Organization — **High systemic relevance; no individual adjudication**

WHO's Global Patient Safety Action Plan 2021–2030 provides a framework for eliminating avoidable harm and strengthening safety across health systems.[REF-016](#ref-016) The alleged mechanism fits especially well with the action plan's learning-system orientation: the safety problem is not only an initial error but the possibility that information architecture impairs subsequent learning.

A suitable WHO-facing output would be a mature, de-identified policy/research paper or patient-safety case mechanism, ideally supported by comparative evidence beyond one case.

## World Medical Association — **High professional-ethics relevance; standard setting rather than discipline**

The WMA International Code of Medical Ethics provides global professional norms, including the duty to ensure accurate and timely medical documentation.[REF-015](#ref-015) The WMA is therefore a natural audience for a general proposition about the prospective ethical obligations of authoritative records. It is not a replacement for the Irish Medical Council.

## UNESCO bioethics structures — **Medium–High normative relevance**

UNESCO's Universal Declaration on Bioethics and Human Rights anchors bioethical principles in dignity, human rights and fundamental freedoms and serves a global standard-setting and deliberative function.[REF-037](#ref-037) UNESCO bioethics structures, including the International Bioethics Committee where appropriate, could recognise the mechanism as a question about participation, vulnerability, professional authority and the information infrastructure of medicine. They do not adjudicate the historical Irish case.

## ISQua — **High quality-and-safety community relevance**

The International Society for Quality in Health Care (ISQua), headquartered in Dublin, describes itself as a global not-for-profit community bringing together clinicians, executives, policymakers, regulators and patient advocates to improve safety and quality through education, knowledge sharing, external evaluation and networks.[REF-038](#ref-038)

The mechanism is a strong fit for quality/safety scholarship because it is testable as a system failure mode: **does an authoritative information state create later non-independent evidence and make double-loop correction less likely?** That can be studied without deciding the individual clinician's culpability.

## Diagnostic-safety community — **High research relevance**

Organisations and researchers focused on diagnostic excellence, diagnostic error and diagnostic safety should care because the proposed mechanism is not ordinary anchoring alone. It describes the **transmission medium** by which a diagnostic partition acquires portability and survives local corrections. This suggests testable interventions: uncertainty tagging, provenance display, patient dissent capture, forced causal-map review after specified contradictions, and independent evidence classification.

## Health informatics — **High research and standards relevance**

IMIA, AMIA, EFMI and related communities are relevant because the mechanism concerns how information structures affect future clinical cognition. Copy-forward research already shows the importance of provenance and identifiable reused content.[REF-017](#ref-017) In a future interoperable digital health system, architecture that obscures provenance or the difference between observation, patient report and inherited interpretation can scale the problem.

## Bioethics and philosophy of medicine — **High conceptual relevance**

The strongest academic audiences include epistemic-injustice scholars, philosophy-of-medicine researchers, clinical ethicists and sociology-of-medicine researchers. The mechanism combines testimonial credibility, hermeneutical framing, professional authority, documentary ontology and organisational learning in a single longitudinal model.

## Journals and scholarly audiences

Priority journal families, depending on the final method and evidence, include:

- **medical ethics / philosophy:** *Journal of Medical Ethics*, *Bioethics*, *Medicine, Health Care and Philosophy*;
- **patient safety / quality:** *BMJ Quality & Safety*, *International Journal for Quality in Health Care*, *Journal of Patient Safety*;
- **diagnostic safety:** *Diagnosis* and diagnostic-excellence research communities;
- **health informatics:** *Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association*, *International Journal of Medical Informatics*, *Applied Clinical Informatics*;
- **health services / sociology:** *Social Science & Medicine*, *Sociology of Health & Illness* where the empirical method supports a sociological contribution.

A live individual complaint dossier is not a journal manuscript. Scholarly publication would require privacy/consent governance, a clear method, disciplined evidential status, competing explanations and a generalisable contribution.

## What can responsibly be claimed about novelty

None of these terms should presently be advertised as unprecedented discoveries. Demonstrating novelty would require a systematic literature review across bioethics, philosophy of medicine, health informatics, sociology of health, diagnostic safety and organisational learning.

What can already be said is narrower: several component mechanisms have strong analogues. Epistemic injustice addresses credibility and interpretive disadvantage.[REF-014](#ref-014) Medical-record sociology establishes the record as constitutive infrastructure.[REF-001](#ref-001) Note-language experiments demonstrate downstream behavioural effects.[REF-002](#ref-002) Copy-forward safety literature highlights provenance and error propagation.[REF-017](#ref-017) Organisational-learning literature distinguishes local/single-loop correction from deeper/double-loop change.[REF-018](#ref-018)

The proposed framework's likely contribution is **integration**: it joins those literatures around one object—the authoritative clinical information state—and makes longitudinal evidence dependence a central ethical concern.

No proposition should be promoted from allegation to fact merely because it appears in multiple downstream notes. The **independence class of supporting evidence must be visible beside the proposition**.

- Medical Council professional conduct/record guidance;

# Part XIII — Communication strategy

The governing rule is **one mechanism, mandate-specific submissions**. Do not send the same undifferentiated complaint everywhere.

| **1. ## A recommended common cover structure

Every submission should begin with five short statements:

1. **What is alleged.** One paragraph describing the information-state mechanism without adjectives about motive.
2. **What is established.** A compact list of authenticated facts.
3. **What remains disputed.** Explicitly identify patient evidence, provenance questions, expert questions and intent questions.
4. **Why this body.** One paragraph mapping the requested examination to its actual mandate.
5. **What is requested.** A finite action the body can lawfully or practically take.

This structure makes the submission harder to dismiss as indiscriminate escalation and harder to overread as claiming more than the evidence proves.

# Part XIV — Ethical falsification

A serious ethics analysis must identify evidence that would undermine the *actual mechanism*, not merely rebut a cruder allegation.

## Evidence that would materially weaken or falsify the mechanism

### 1. Adequate contemporaneous relational assessment

The mechanism would be substantially weakened if authenticated 2017 records, testimony or other evidence showed that Neurology explicitly investigated whether the painful feet, gait/posture, increased tone/tension, dystonic-looking lower limb and structural pathology were related; considered mixed mechanisms; documented why the relationship was or was not neurologically persuasive; and made a proportionate follow-up plan.

This would directly attack the premise that “unclear” represented incompletely investigated uncertainty rather than earned specialist uncertainty.

### 2. Accurate provenance of the disputed phrase cluster

If the source record established that “burning,” “leg pain,” and “markedly divorced” were accurate, context-preserving representations of the patient's own history and intended causal distinctions—and not recodings introduced by the service—the microconstruction allegation would materially contract.

Similarly, if alcohol/neuropathy reasoning were proportionately presented as one differential among genuinely balanced alternatives, the alleged asymmetric architecture would weaken.

### 3. Protest appropriately answered and preserved

If evidence showed that the pre-discharge Protest was documented in another authoritative location, clearly responded to, incorporated into the plan, and available to downstream clinicians, the “warning-label removal” component would fail or become much narrower.

### 4. Balanced future for dystonia

If the final synthesis or linked plan explicitly preserved an equivalent “dystonia/feet relationship” question, arranged follow-up or testing, and made clear that the foot problem remained neurologically open, the allegation of asymmetric explanatory futures would be seriously weakened.

### 5. Independent downstream reconstruction

The recursive-validation claim would be weakened if later clinicians independently returned to raw observations, deliberately disregarded inherited labels, re-examined the causal map, and—after genuine comparison—still supported the original partition. Such evidence would be especially powerful if generated by specialists unaware of the contested formulation.

### 6. Proven independence of downstream evidence

If apparently corroborative later evidence can be shown to have been independently generated rather than inherited, the “documentary descendants” and endogenous-validation components shrink. The issue is not date but dependence.

### 7. Independent governance reconstruction

If a prior institutional review can demonstrate that it reconstructed primary evidence, tested the protest allegation without reasoning from its absence, distinguished independent from derivative records, obtained appropriate specialty expertise, evaluated alternative explanations and directly tested the alleged information architecture, then the claim that governance recursively validated the chart through its descendants would be weakened.

### 8. Temporal evidence inconsistent with the alleged sequence

Audit logs or version history could falsify important causal links. For example, if the challenged final architecture was completed and immutable before the alleged Protest, and a later addendum or separate record appropriately captured the protest, the claimed post-notice construction would require substantial revision.

### 9. Evidence that later contradiction did force global reconstruction

If the record shows an explicit later event at which clinicians reopened the founding partition, restored the historical feet–dystonia question, reassessed prior peripheral explanations, and communicated the revised information state to primary care and other downstream services, “constrained corrigibility” would no longer describe the whole trajectory.

## What does **not** falsify the mechanism

The following facts may be clinically important but do not, by themselves, answer the mechanism:

- structural foot pathology existed;
- Parkinsonism existed;
- alcohol exposure was genuine;
- neuropathy was medically conceivable;
- individual wording had an innocent possible explanation;
- later clinicians acted in good faith;
- local symptom labels or treatment plans changed;
- the longitudinal record appears coherent;
- an earlier review concluded care was acceptable without demonstrating independent reconstruction.

Those facts can reduce probability or moral gravity when placed in context. They do not logically establish that the founding information state accurately represented the work done or that downstream evidence was independent.

# Part XV — Final ethical judgments

## 1. Individual-clinician ethics

**If substantially established**, the alleged conduct would represent a potentially serious problem of professional epistemic integrity, record stewardship and patient participation—not merely a mistaken differential diagnosis. The seriousness would depend on role, authorship, control of the final record, the clinical reasonableness of the 2017 work-up, foreseeability of downstream reliance, the materiality of the Protest, and whether any misleading architecture was negligent, reckless or intentional.

Intent is an aggravating variable, not a necessary element of every ethical wrong. A record can produce foreseeable epistemic harm through careless compression, unexamined assumptions or institutional routines. Conversely, allegations of dishonesty require evidence of consciousness and should never be inferred solely from the document's effect.

## 2. Documentary ethics

An authoritative specialist record creates **prospective ethical obligations** because it becomes an instrument of future care. Those obligations include:

- accurate representation of what is known and not known;
- clear distinction between observation, patient report and clinician inference;
- preservation of material unresolved uncertainty;
- accurate attribution of patient statements;
- preservation of material dissent or challenge where it changes the meaning of apparent closure;
- proportionate representation of competing explanations;
- provenance sufficient to evaluate inherited information; and
- correction, qualification or supplementation when later evidence materially changes the epistemic state.

The ethical object is therefore not only the “medical record” as archive. It is the **information state exported to future decision-makers**.

## 3. Longitudinal-care ethics

When later evidence repeatedly crosses a founding diagnostic partition—new dystonic manifestations, treatment response, changed mechanism, genotype, or other materially integrative evidence—the duty is not satisfied by local re-labelling alone. At some threshold, continuity of care requires **global model review**: asking whether the original causal separation should itself be redrawn and whether downstream clinicians need an updated synthesis.

This is the clinical expression of double-loop learning. It does not mean every new symptom requires wholesale rediagnosis; it means accumulating cross-partition evidence must eventually trigger a review at the level where the partition was created.

## 4. Institutional ethics

Health systems have an ethical responsibility to detect self-reinforcing information states. A just culture should protect staff from simplistic hindsight blame while refusing a second, opposite error: treating longitudinal documentary consistency as independent proof when the founding record helped create that consistency.

Competent institutional review should therefore include provenance analysis, independent evidence classification, primary-source reconstruction, patient testimony, appropriately blinded clinical expertise where feasible, alternative explanations, and explicit falsification criteria. The aim is neither prosecution nor exoneration by narrative inertia. It is system learning.

## 5. Societal significance

**Yes—conditionally.** The proposed mechanism identifies a plausible generalisable patient-safety problem at the intersection of diagnostic safety, medical-record integrity, health informatics, epistemic justice and organisational learning. Modern healthcare increasingly depends on portable and interoperable summaries. That increases both the value of accurate synthesis and the scale of harm when epistemic status is misrepresented.

The case should not be promoted as a general theory until its own evidential foundation is stronger. But the mechanism is sufficiently coherent, connected to existing literatures and operationalisable to warrant serious scholarly and patient-safety examination.

**14. Peer-reviewed medical ethics, safety, diagnostic and informatics journals.** Asked to scrutinise a publication-grade, de-identified and falsifiable account—not to serve as an alternative complaints tribunal.

# Overall conclusion

The strongest version of the allegation is ethically coherent. It describes a possible **information-governance failure in clinical medicine** in which specialist authority does more than reach a diagnosis: it creates an epistemic object that travels. If that object makes an inadequately investigated relationship look sufficiently investigated, removes a contemporaneous warning that closure was disputed, gives one hypothesis a durable future while underdeveloping another observed relationship, and then helps generate the downstream documentation later used to defend it, the resulting wrong is both individual and systemic.

The ethical response must be equally disciplined. The mechanism should not be diluted into “doctors sometimes make mistakes,” but neither should it be insulated from falsification. The next evidential step is not broader accusation. It is **independent reconstruction**: source provenance, protest evidence, contemporaneous clinical expert review, and a dependency map separating genuinely new evidence from documentary descendants.

Only after that reconstruction should the issue widen in stages—from provider and HSE governance, to any properly framed professional/data/administrative jurisdiction, and then to de-identified scholarly and policy scrutiny. That sequence preserves both seriousness and fairness.
